Project overview

The product

WiSpend is an offline-first Android expense tracker built with React Native. You type what you bought and how much, and a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) model suggests the category from your own past spending, with a confidence score you can accept or change.

It is made for students on an allowance, young professionals, and low-income earners who want to see where their money goes without linking a bank account.

Project duration

April to May 2026, in seven one-week sprints.

The problem

Logging every expense by hand is slow, and sorting each one into a category is tedious, so most people quit within weeks. Nothing warns them before their money runs out.

The goal

Make logging take seconds, sort expenses automatically while showing why, warn users before the budget runs out, and keep their data private and usable offline.

Understanding the user

User research summary

I planned a five-question survey and three short interviews around how people track spending today and what makes them stop. Going in, I assumed users mainly wanted charts. The research pointed somewhere else: speed of logging and being able to trust and correct the automatic category mattered more.

Note: the interview responses below are sample responses I wrote to test the survey and plan the design. They are not answers from real participants.

Sample response 1"If I just type what I bought and the app figures out the rest, I'd probably use it more."
Sample response 2"If I could just log something in under 30 seconds, that would be ideal. Speed is everything for me."
Sample response 3"If the confidence score is shown and I can override it, then yes, I'd trust it."

Pain points

1. Logging is slowTrackers that ask for too many fields get abandoned. WiSpend asks for three: description, amount, and date.
2. Sorting is tediousPicking a category for every expense is a chore. The KNN model suggests one with a single tap to accept.
3. Hard to trust automationA guess with no explanation feels risky. Every prediction shows a confidence score and a Change button.
4. No early warningPeople only notice overspending at the end of the month. WiSpend sends alerts as the budget runs low.

Starting the design

Wireframes

I sketched the wireframes directly in Figma on April 1 and 2, 2026, starting with the four screens users visit most: Login, Dashboard, Add Expense, and History. I kept Add Expense to a single screen so logging never takes more than one step, and put the prediction right under the form so users see the result without scrolling.

View the wireframes and mockups in Figma (opens in a new tab)

User flow

  1. Register
  2. Save your UID
  3. Dashboard
  4. Add expense
  5. KNN predicts the category
  6. Accept or change
  7. Dashboard updates

If there is no past data yet, the user picks the category by hand, and the model learns from it.

How the KNN works

  1. The description is matched against a keyword dictionary for each category.
  2. The amount is compared against the user's own spending history.
  3. The app measures how close the new expense is to every past one.
  4. The three closest past expenses vote on the category.
  5. The share of votes that agree becomes the confidence score.

Usability study: findings

I ran two rounds of testing on an Android phone. Each round had one tester, and both asked to stay anonymous.

Round 1: classmate tester
  1. The Terms of Service felt alarming, with red warning boxes stacked everywhere.
  2. The UID warning needed to be clearer at the moment users receive their UID.
  3. The overall interface needed a cleaner look.
Round 2: mentor tester
  1. Nothing warned users once they spent more than their budget.
  2. The graphs and categories needed to be easier to read.
  3. One fixed income was awkward. Users needed a monthly income plus a way to add extra income such as gifts.

Refining the design

Mockups: before and after the usability study

Round 1 change: I rewrote the Terms of Service as plain numbered sections and moved the strong warnings to the UID screen, right after sign-up, where users actually need them.

Round 2 changes: budget alerts now cover the whole month, the income setup has a monthly income plus extra income, and the categories got clearer labels and summaries.

Accessibility considerations

1. Not color aloneThe confidence score is shown as a number and a bar, and every category has an icon and a text label, so no meaning depends on color.
2. Light and dark modeEvery screen works in both themes, and the choice is saved between sessions for comfortable use at night.
3. Clear words and big targetsLarge, labeled buttons like Accept and Save, and alerts that state the exact amount left in plain language.

Takeaways

Impact

"Out of all the students that presented us an app regarding finances that are similar to WiSpend, your project stands out and is the best one I've reviewed."Panel reviewer

What I learned

  • Where a warning appears matters as much as what it says. The same UID warning felt scary in the terms and helpful on the UID screen.
  • Showing why the model guessed a category builds trust, so users accept good guesses and fix the rest.
  • Designing local-first made the app reliable even without internet, which also made demos safer.
  • Setting up a shared theme early would have made dark mode much faster to add across all screens.
  • Hosting details, like Railway clearing files on restart, need planning before launch.

Next steps

1. Recurring expensesTemplates for things paid every month, like load or rent, so they log themselves.
2. Month-to-month trendsCompare spending across months so users can see if they are improving.
3. Smarter predictionsA larger Filipino keyword dictionary so new accounts get accurate categories sooner.
